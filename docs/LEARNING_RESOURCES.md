# Learning resources for Person B — Martian Map
Study in this order. Each resource answers a type of judge question.

## 1. Start here — Mars basics (Day 1, ~1 hour)
**NASA Science: Mars Facts** — https://science.nasa.gov/mars/facts/
- What to learn: thin CO2 atmosphere, temperature range (about 20 C to -153 C),
  dust storms that can cover much of the planet, water today = ice under the
  surface and briny flows.
- Judge questions this answers: "Why is Mars hard to survive on?"
  "Why is temperature in your score?"

## 2. Explore Mars like your app will (Day 1-2, ~1 hour, hands-on)
**NASA Mars Trek** — https://marstrek.jpl.nasa.gov
- Google Earth for Mars: 2D/3D views, elevation (MOLA) layers, bookmarks for
  rover/lander sites. NASA teams used it in landing-site selection work.
- Do this: find each of our 5 sites in Trek. Check the elevation layer.
- Judge questions: "Where did your map data / coordinates come from?"
  "What does elevation have to do with landing?" (Lower = more atmosphere
  to slow down in.)

## 3. Water — the heart of your score (Day 2-3, most important)
**NASA JPL: SWIM — Subsurface Water Ice Mapping**
- Article: https://www.jpl.nasa.gov/news/nasa-is-locating-ice-on-mars-with-this-new-map/
- Map image page: https://science.nasa.gov/photojournal/swim-map-shows-subsurface-water-ice-on-mars/
- What to learn: water ice = drinking, oxygen, rocket fuel. Ice is stable on
  the surface only at cold high latitudes, so scientists hunt BURIED ice in
  the mid-latitudes — far enough north for ice, close enough to the equator
  for warmth and sunlight. SWIM combines MRO, Mars Odyssey and Mars Global
  Surveyor data, equator to 60 N.
- Judge questions: "Why is water 30% of your score?" "Why not just land at
  the pole where all the ice is?" (Answer: too cold, too little sunlight.)

**NASA JPL: Where Should Future Astronauts Land on Mars? Follow the Water**
- https://www.jpl.nasa.gov/news/where-should-future-astronauts-land-on-mars-follow-the-water
- The astronaut-landing "sweet spot": northern mid-latitudes, e.g. Arcadia
  Planitia and Deuteronilus Mensae — middle ground between ice (poles) and
  warmth/sunlight (equator), plus low elevation for landing.
- This article IS the real-world version of our Survival Score. Quote it.

## 4. Site-by-site deep dives (Day 3-5, one site per session)
Use the source_url in sites.csv for each site, plus:
- **JPL mission press kits** (search "press kit" + mission name on jpl.nasa.gov)
  — Jezero/Perseverance and Elysium/InSight kits gave us our coordinates
  and InSight's temperature range (-100 C to -20 C).
- **HiRISE (University of Arizona)** — http://hirise.lpl.arizona.edu
  The sharpest camera on Mars (on MRO). Used to scout safe landing sites.
  Search it for close-up images of our sites — great for slides, and it
  teaches you what "hazardous terrain" actually looks like.
- Judge questions: "Tell me about this site." "Why did Gale get terrain 3
  but Elysium get 5?" — your answers live here.

## 5. Elevation & terrain data (Week 2, when Pogo needs data)
**USGS Astrogeology — Mars MOLA DEM**
- https://astrogeology.usgs.gov/search/map/mars_mgs_mola_dem_463m
- MOLA (on Mars Global Surveyor) made 600+ million elevation measurements.
  This is the standard source for "is it flat / how high is it?"
- Judge question: "What data would you add next?" (Answer: MOLA elevation
  as a layer, instead of our manual terrain scores.)

## Study rule for Person B
For every fact you learn, write it as: FACT -> SO WHAT for survival.
Example: "Utopia has a buried ice deposit as big as Lake Superior" ->
"so an astronaut base there would not need to import water."
That second sentence is what judges score.
